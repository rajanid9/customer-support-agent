## Summary

Renewables added a record 692 GW in 2025 (IRENA, via secondary coverage), reaching 5,149 GW, about 49% of installed power capacity. Solar (511 GW) and wind (159 GW) dominate additions. Onshore wind and solar PV are the cheapest new sources in most markets. Geothermal is growing, with enhanced systems reaching commercial operation. Tidal and marine energy remain small and costly. Bioenergy is a small share of additions and its sustainability is contested. Fusion is an emerging energy technology, not a currently deployed renewable source: no fusion plant supplies electricity today, and company timelines are self-reported and have historically slipped. This report is INCOMPLETE. Coverage is adequate only for geothermal and fusion. Solar, wind, tidal/marine, biomass and integration/policy have significant gaps. Nearly all figures come from search summaries and secondary coverage, and no primary IRENA, IEA, IHA or GWEC report was opened.

## Findings

- **GLOBAL CAPACITY:** IRENA (via secondary news coverage) reports a record 692 GW of renewables added in 2025 (+15.5%), reaching 5,149 GW, about 49% of installed power capacity. Renewables were 85.6% of all capacity added. Solar added 511 GW (~75% of additions) and wind 159 GW. Bioenergy added only 3.4 GW. Asia accounted for 513.3 GW (74.2%). Caveat: the quoted 96.8% share for solar plus wind is described as a share of net renewable additions, and the wording is not fully reconciled with the 692 GW total.
- **SOLAR (PV technology):** The highest independently tabulated silicon-perovskite tandem efficiency found is LONGi's 35.2% (Solar Cell Efficiency Tables v68, May 2026). LONGi's announced 35.5% is a company claim not confirmed as independently certified. LONGi also reports certified tandem modules at 31.4% and 29.4%, and larger-area cells at 34.3% (261 cm2) and 32.2% (274 cm2). One source notes perovskite records have not yet translated into shipped products, so durability and commercialization are open issues. No evidence was gathered for a full comparison of silicon, thin-film and perovskite on cost and durability.
- **SOLAR (cost):** Per IRENA via PV Tech (the one page opened), global weighted-average solar PV LCOE was US$44/MWh in 2025, up slightly from US$43, while total installed cost fell 6% to US$667/kW, the lowest of any technology profiled. This indicates a plateau in LCOE rather than continued decline; the cause of the divergence was not given. Regional solar LCOE: India US$35, China US$36, Brazil US$37, Germany US$65, Europe average US$59. IRENA projects PV installation costs falling about 40% over 2026-2035; this is a projection, not an observed trend. Leading markets are evidenced only by Asia's 74.2% share of 2025 renewable additions.
- **SOLAR (gaps):** No evidence was gathered on concentrated solar power and thermal storage, PV recycling, supply chain, or intermittency and grid integration specific to solar. These objectives are unfulfilled.
- **WIND (offshore):** GWEC's Global Offshore Wind Report 2026 (via secondary coverage) gives 92.5 GW of offshore capacity at end-2025, with 9.3 GW added in 2025 (third-strongest year) and more than 50 GW under construction. GWEC expects annual installs to double in 2026 and exceed 50 GW/yr by 2035, reaching about 420 GW cumulative by 2035 (a forecast). Floating offshore wind stalled: no new capacity in 2025, the first such year since 2015, leaving 278 MW cumulative, which GWEC attributes to macroeconomic and policy headwinds. Pipeline examples: 16.8 MW in Japan and 65 MW across three French projects.
- **WIND (cost):** IRENA 2025 figures via secondary coverage (SolarQuarter): global weighted-average LCOE about US$33/MWh onshore wind (cheapest new source, down ~4%) and US$78/MWh offshore wind (down ~3%). These LCOE values were not confirmed on the opened PV Tech page. Onshore wind installed cost was US$976/kW (-8%), per the opened PV Tech page. Regional spread (secondary): China onshore US$27 and offshore US$49/MWh versus US$141/MWh for US offshore wind. IRENA projects onshore wind installation costs falling about 20% over 2026-2035 (projection).
- **WIND (gaps):** No evidence was gathered on turbine and rotor size trends, airborne concepts, onshore capacity factors, wildlife, siting and community impacts, or supply chain, grid connection and permitting constraints. These objectives are unfulfilled.
- **GEOTHERMAL (capacity):** ThinkGeoEnergy reports 17,173 MW of global geothermal power capacity at end-2025, up 223 MW in the year (including data corrections). The US leads with 3,953 MW, followed by Indonesia (2,742 MW, fastest-growing large market), then Mexico, Italy, Iceland and Japan. The top ten countries hold over 93% of capacity. Baseload advantage is only implicit in the sources and was not directly evidenced.
- **GEOTHERMAL (enhanced and closed-loop):** Fervo Energy's Cape Station (Utah) declared commercial operation around Sept 30/Oct 1, 2026 (sources give slightly different dates), with the first GeoBlock at 33 MW net. Fervo calls it the first greenfield enhanced geothermal project to reach this stage (company claim). Phase I is about 100 MW (units 2 and 3 due Q1 2027); Phase II adds about 400 MW, targeted for 2028. Fervo reports 658 MW of contracted PPAs, a 3 GW framework with Google, and $421.4M Phase I project debt (company-reported). For closed-loop designs, Eavor-Loop techno-economic modelling finds lateral drilling cost dominates economics; LCOE below $70/MWh needs a gradient of at least 60 C/km, a discount rate below 9% and drilling below $400/m. These are modelled thresholds, not achieved costs. Eavor says its first 8 of 12 laterals at Geretsried, Germany took over 100 days and the last four took about 50% less time (company-reported); the site was reported as about to come online in Nov 2025, and current status is unconfirmed.
- **GEOTHERMAL (induced seismicity and risk):** Hydraulic stimulation in EGS can reactivate faults; the Basel and Pohang projects were curtailed or ended after damaging or felt events. An expert elicitation gave M>=3 annual exceedance probabilities from 0.2% to 95-100%, showing deep uncertainty. No source addressed seismicity for closed-loop designs; the idea that closed loops avoid stimulation-driven seismicity is inference only. Conventional hydrothermal resource risk was not evidenced.
- **GEOTHERMAL (direct use and heat pumps):** Lund and Toth (World Geothermal Congress 2020) estimated global direct-use capacity at 107,727 MWt at end-2019 (+52% since 2015), about 1,020,887 TJ/yr, with heat pumps about 58.8% of energy used. A report citing 2023 data gives about 126,000 MWt and about 1.28 million TJ/yr across 88+ countries, with ground-source heat pumps about 72% of capacity. These differ in year and method and mix energy and capacity metrics, so they should not be read as a trend. US figures (2020 IEA Geothermal TCP): 485 MWt direct use and 20.7 GW heat pumps. Direct-use thermal capacity is several times the roughly 17 GW of electric capacity, but thermal and electrical capacity are not interchangeable.
- **TIDAL AND MARINE:** Sihwa Lake (South Korea) is a 254 MW tidal barrage operating since 2011 and is still the world's largest tidal plant. MeyGen (Scotland) is the largest tidal stream project, with a 6 MW Phase 1 in operation (four 1.5 MW turbines) and a 28 MW Phase 2 that won a UK CfD (AR4) at GBP 178.54/MWh with a 2027 target; the site is licensed for up to 398 MW. The MeyGen details come from a 2023 write-up and may be outdated. The strike price is high compared with IRENA's US$78/MWh global offshore wind LCOE, but the two are different measures and currencies, so the comparison is indicative only. Tidal has struggled to compete with falling wind costs. No current evidence was found on wave energy, OTEC, tidal lagoons, ecosystem impacts or durability.
- **BIOENERGY (capacity and fuels):** Bioenergy added only 3.4 GW in 2025 (IRENA, secondary). A secondhand report of a 2026 IEA analysis says sustainable fuel use could quadruple by 2035, led by aviation and shipping, with about USD 1.5 trillion of investment needed; this was not verified. Pathway's Port Arthur BECCS project (about 1.9 Mt CO2/yr removal) was slated to start construction in early 2026, but this was not confirmed. EIA cut its 2026 outlook for 'other' biofuels (including SAF) to 40,000 b/d from 50,000 b/d, though the dating is uncertain. Solid biomass, biogas and heat/power roles were not evidenced.
- **BIOENERGY (lifecycle and land use):** A peer-reviewed PNNL study (2021) estimated induced land-use-change emissions for 17 ICAO-recognized SAF pathways at 25-year amortization, ranging from -58.5 g CO2e/MJ (US miscanthus alcohol-to-jet) to +34.6 (Malaysia/Indonesia palm oil HEFA). Fifteen of 17 had lower full lifecycle intensity than petroleum jet fuel. WRI (an advocacy group) argues crop-based SAF displaces food crops and drives land conversion. The IEA's 2018 Renewables report acknowledges land-use changeand residential biomass pollution concerns. The disagreement stems from modelling choices (land-use accounting, time horizon, feedstock, region). The PNNL result covers only land-use change for SAF and does not settle the carbon-neutrality debate for solid biomass. The evidence is dated (2018, 2021).
- **FUSION (status):** Fusion is an emerging energy technology, not a currently deployed renewable source; no fusion plant supplies power to a grid today. ITER has a new baseline with deuterium-tritium operation in 2039 instead of 2035 and about EUR 5 billion in added cost. Commonwealth Fusion Systems' SPARC is reported roughly 75-80% complete(single source), with operations and net energy gain targeted for 2027 (slipped from 2026); CFS has raised about $1B more, and its ARC plant is targeted for the early 2030s. Helion said in Feb 2026 that its Polaris prototype was the first private machine to demonstrate measurable D-T fusion, at 150 million C. It is building a 50 MWe plant in Malaga, Washington, targeted for 2028, and was reportedly valued at $15.5B in June 2026. NIF's record yield is 8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ of laser energy (April 2025), up from3.15 MJ at the 2022 ignition; this is target gain, still far from net electricity. Company timelines are self-reported and have historically slipped.
- **FUSION (confinement approaches; explainer-level sources, including Wikipedia):** Magnetic confinement holds low-density plasma for a long time. Tokamaks (e.g. ITER) are toroidal and use external coils plus a plasma-driven current; they have the best track record on temperature and confinement time but the current is a source of instabilities. Stellarators (e.g. Wendelstein 7-X) use only twisted external coils and need no plasma current, removing those instabilities but making coil geometry much harder to engineer; they are less mature. Inertial confinement (e.g. NIF) compresses a small D-T pellet with lasers so fusion happens before the plasma disassembles, using very high density for a very short time. Magnetized target fusion is a hybrid: magnetically insulated fuel at lower density is rapidly compressed ('magneto-inertial fusion' is the broader term). The Lawson criterion frames the density versus confinement-time trade-off.
- **FUSION (fuel, materials, classification):** Global tritium inventory is estimated at about 50 kg, mostly from Canadian CANDU reactors, and one source says a 100 MWe magnetic-confinement plant would consume about 17 kg/yr, so plants must breed tritium from lithium. Lithium-6, beryllium, tungsten and magnet materials such as yttrium and niobium were flagged as supply concerns, and neutron damage limits wall lifetime. These apply to both tokamaks and stellarators. Sources are conference slides and industry explainers, so treat figures as estimates. Whether fusion counts as renewable is unresolved: no authoritative classification was found. One abstract calls it a 'virtually unlimited renewable' source, which describes potential, not regulatory status. Any classification needs a jurisdiction-specific check (e.g. EU rules, US state standards). It can be described as a prospective clean, low-carbon option, which is a different question from renewable classification.
- **STORAGE:** IHA's World Hydropower Outlook 2026 (released 23 June 2026; secondary coverage, an industry body framing hydropower as flexibility) reports 201 GW of pumped storage, a record 11.6 GW commissioned in 2025, 243 GW under construction and a 621 GW pipeline. Rystad (consultancy, secondary and unverified) expects more than 130 GW / 350 GWh of battery additions in 2026 and reportedly says battery capacity has overtaken pumped hydro; it is unclear whether this is GW or GWh, and the claim is single-source. The IEA (2024, via pv-magazine) called for a sixfold expansion of global storage capacity by 2030, with batteries about 90% of the increase; this is a scenario, not an outcome. The older IEA 2021 forecast that pumped hydro would stay about 3x larger than batteries is outdated. No evidence on hydrogen storage was found.
- **COMPETITIVENESS AND INVESTMENT:** IRENA (via PV Tech and SolarQuarter) says over 90% of newly commissioned utility-scale renewables in 2025 were cheaper than the cheapestnew fossil alternative, with USD 480B in avoided fuel costs; gas LCOE approached USD 100/MWh in Italy, Germany and Japan. IEA World Energy Investment 2026 (released May 29; confirmed via the IEA page snippet and multiple secondary reports, not a full read) forecasts about $3.4T total energy investment in 2026 (+5% real), with about $2.2T for 'clean' categories (renewables, nuclear, grids, storage, low-emissions fuels, efficiency, electrification) and about $1.2T for fossil fuels. The IEA 'clean' category isbroader than renewables. BNEF separately reported a record $2.3T of energy-transition investment in 2025 under a different definition, so the two are not directly comparable.
- **CRITICAL MINERALS (IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026, secondhand):** battery demand grew over 35% in 2025 to above 1.5 TWh, with storage a major driver and LFP gaining share. Energy accounted for about 75% of demand growth for key minerals. Critical-mineral investment fell 9% in 2025, which the IEA attributes to high financing costs, and it warns of possible bottlenecks for lithium, nickel, cobalt and rare earths.
- **POLICY GAPS:** No evidence was gathered on major policies, incentives, climate targets, permitting, equity or hydrogen. These objectives are unfulfilled.
